Settlement reconciliation that refuses
to guess.

One bank credit is rarely one payment. It is a batch — net of fees, GST on those fees, refunds and holds — settled two days late under a UTR that may not have survived into the narration. Kosh ties it back, types every failure it cannot resolve, and declines rather than inventing a match.

00

The problem

A single credit of ₹4,87,213 is 312 payments, minus fees, minus GST, minus two refunds, settled T+2.

Finance teams do this in Excel. The obvious fix is subset-sum — find the rows that add up to the credit — and it is silently wrong: past about twenty rows there are more subsets than achievable paise values, so spurious exact matches become certain.

Kosh constrains the pool with a structural prior first, attaches a collision risk to every arithmetic match, and declines the ones it cannot justify.

01

The cascade floor

Every bank line rests on the tier that actually resolved it; cube height is the amount. The crowded top slab and the near-empty adjudicator slab below it are the whole argument — almost nothing needed a model.

02

The tally

Recall is how much got matched. The false-match rate is how much got matched wrongly, and it is the number to read first: a missed match costs an analyst ten minutes, a wrong match misstates the books and nobody finds out.

03

Where the model was used

Every tier below the adjudicator is deterministic and costs nothing but CPU. The model sees a closed list of candidates the engine already computed, and can only pick one or abstain — enforced in code, not in the prompt. Switch it off to see the floor.
